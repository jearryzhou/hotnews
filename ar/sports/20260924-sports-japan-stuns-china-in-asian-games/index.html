Japan Stuns China in Asian Games Table Tennis, Women Answer with Gold

24 سبتمبر 2026 3 دقائق قراءة 👁

Table tennis occupies a special place in China’s sporting heart. The fast-paced racket game is widely treated as the country’s national sport, and at major championships anything short of gold counts as seismic news. So when day five of the 2026 Asian Games arrived in Japan’s Aichi prefecture and its host city Nagoya, few spectators anywhere in the arena expected the twist the sport had in store: a men’s team final in which Japan edged China 3-2 to claim a title many assumed was bound for the Chinese bench.

The Asian Games, a continental multi-sport festival held every four years, sit among the world’s largest sporting events, and table tennis traditionally anchors China’s gold rush. This time the final swung back and forth before Japan clinched the decisive point. The sharpest pain belonged to Wen Ruibo, a rising young player who stepped onto the biggest stage of his career and lost both of his matches. In the unforgiving arithmetic of a team contest, those two defeats decided the color of the medal.

Wen did not hide afterward. Losing two points in a final, he admitted, was a reality he found hard to accept, but his verdict on himself was blunt: the gap is still there, and he needs more training, more experience and more big matches. For a young athlete, the evening may prove a costly yet formative lesson on the road to future championships.

Men’s head coach Wang Hao, himself a former world champion who understands the weight of Chinese expectations better than most, struck a measured tone. He congratulated Japan on the victory, insisted his three players had fought with everything they had, and reminded listeners that results do not always follow form. The squad, he said, would review the loss carefully, with singles events starting the very next day offering an immediate chance at redemption — an opportunity, in his words, to turn a bad thing into a good one.

Hours later, China’s women restored the familiar order. Their team final against Japan ended 3-0, a clean sweep that secured a sixth consecutive Asian Games women’s team crown, a run of dominance stretching across decades. Sun Yingsha, one of the game’s brightest stars, set the tone from the front, while Wang Manyu later described her own match as a grinding uphill fight — in her colorful phrasing, a genuinely hard nut to crack.

Sun said the team had walked into the final with a fighting mindset, taking nothing for granted against a Japanese side they clearly respect. Head coach Ma Lin, a former Olympic champion who now leads the women’s program, declared that his players had handed in a thoroughly satisfying answer sheet — a Chinese sporting idiom meaning the job was done properly. The contrast with the men’s dressing room could hardly have been starker.

Wider context softens the blow for Chinese fans. After five days of competition, China’s delegation still sits comfortably atop the overall medal table with 80 golds, 30 silvers and 20 bronzes, a reminder of the country’s depth across dozens of sports. And table tennis is far from finished at these Games: the individual events begin on Thursday, giving the men a rapid opportunity to answer their critics on the same tables.

For international viewers, the day offered a vivid snapshot of where world table tennis stands. Japan’s men, blending youthful energy with big-match nerve, have genuinely closed the distance on their mighty neighbors, while China’s women remain a class apart. The next chapter arrives quickly, with singles medals on the line in Nagoya — and a wounded champion, as every rival knows, is the most dangerous kind of favorite.
