Japan Ends 36-Year Chinese Reign in Asian Games Table Tennis Final

24 सित॰ 2026 3 मिनट पढ़ें 👁

For 36 years, the men’s team table tennis final at the Asian Games ended the same way: with a Chinese gold medal. This week, in Nagoya, Japan, that streak finally snapped. Playing in front of a home crowd, Japan overturned a 2-1 deficit to beat China 3-2 in a gripping final, winning the men’s team title for the first time in six decades and producing one of the biggest results in the sport in recent memory.

Table tennis occupies a unique place in China. The country has ruled international competition for generations, and the Asian Games — a continental multi-sport event held every four years — have been no exception. Before this week, the Chinese men’s team had gone 36 years without missing the top step of the podium. Japan’s triumph, its first men’s team gold at the Games in 60 years, therefore sent a jolt through the sport worldwide.

The final itself delivered plenty of drama. In a format where the first team to three match wins takes the tie, China edged ahead 2-1, leaving Japan needing to win both remaining matches against opponents who rarely loosen their grip at this level. Instead, the hosts held their nerve. Japanese coach Shinya Kishikawa singled out Tomokazu Harimoto, Japan’s most prominent player, for his concentration under pressure, praising an outstanding performance.

Kishikawa did not hide his emotions afterwards. ‘I am truly happy that we were able to beat China, and to win here at a Games held in Japan,’ he said. ‘The men’s team has always aimed to beat China. What makes me happiest is that we finally achieved that goal.’ He added that fighting back from 1-2 down against a Chinese side made the victory all the harder — and all the sweeter.

China’s head coach Wang Hao, a former world champion himself, was gracious in defeat. ‘First, congratulations to our opponents,’ he said. ‘The three players who went out there today gave everything they had. Matches cannot always go the way we expect.’ He stressed that the squad would review the loss carefully and quickly turn the page, with the individual singles events beginning the next day — an opportunity, he suggested, to turn a bad result into a good one.

Youngster Wen Ruibo, who lost both of his matches in the final, was blunt about his own display. ‘Losing two points in a final is a reality I find hard to accept,’ he said. ‘But the facts are the facts. The gap is still there. I need to keep training, keep experiencing matches like this, and keep giving everything.’ His candor reflected a broader willingness within the Chinese camp to confront the result head-on rather than explain it away.

Hours earlier, China’s women’s team had offered a reminder of the country’s enduring strength, sweeping Japan 3-0 to claim a sixth consecutive Asian Games team title. Sun Yingsha, one of the world’s leading players, said the squad had focused simply on fighting for every point. Wang Manyu described her own match as a hard bone that had to be chewed through, while coach Ma Lin said the team had delivered a thoroughly satisfying answer in the team event.

Beyond table tennis, China’s overall Games remain dominant: after five days of competition, the delegation leads the medal table with 80 golds, 30 silvers and 20 bronzes. Day six brings swimming finals and a chance for equestrian dressage team gold. Yet the table tennis result will linger longest. For years, critics have argued that Chinese dominance dulls the sport’s global appeal. Japan’s breakthrough suggests the gap at the very top is narrowing — and that a genuine rivalry, long predicted, may finally be arriving.
